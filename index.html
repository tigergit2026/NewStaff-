About This Training

This training is designed to help new staff understand how privacy principles apply to news media work. It covers the Australian Press Council's Statement of Privacy Principles, the Australian Privacy Principles (APPs), and practical guidance on how to apply these principles in everyday reporting and editing work.

Why It Matters: Privacy is not just a legal requirement. It's a core professional value. Respecting privacy builds public trust, protects sources, and upholds the integrity of journalism.

Module 1: Privacy Principles

All staff must understand and apply privacy principles in their daily work. These principles come from two main sources:

  • Australian Press Council (APC) — Statement of Privacy Principles
    Applies specifically to news media work.
  • Australian Privacy Principles (APPs)
    Set out in the Privacy Act 1988 (Cth), apply to how we handle personal information.
1

Public Interest Test

Privacy is not an absolute right. Publication is justified when there is a clear public interest that outweighs an individual's right to privacy.

2

Children and Young People

Extra care must be taken when publishing material about children. Their right to privacy is stronger than that of adults.

3

Health and Personal Tragedy

Avoid intrusive reporting on people experiencing personal tragedy or serious health issues, unless there is a clear public interest justification.

4

Family and Private Life

Respect individuals' right to keep their family and private life private. Requires a strong public interest justification.

5

Harassment and Pursuit

Do not harass, pursue, or photograph individuals in a way that invades their privacy, especially in places where they have a reasonable expectation of privacy.

6

Deception

Avoid the use of deception or surreptitious methods to obtain material, unless there is a strong public interest justification and no other way to obtain the information.

7

Consent and Awareness

Where possible, seek consent before publishing private information. Individuals should be aware that information they provide may be published.

8

Correction and Response

Offer individuals who are the subject of critical privacy-related coverage a reasonable opportunity to respond, and correct any inaccuracies promptly.

Module 2: Respect Privacy When Publishing

Everyone has a right to privacy. The media serves the public interest by publishing information that matters. But publication must be justified by a clear public interest that outweighs the individual's right to privacy.

What Is "Public Interest"?

  • Exposes wrongdoing, corruption, or behaviour that affects the public
  • Protects public health or safety
  • Informs public debate on matters of importance to the community
  • Prevents the public from being misled by someone's actions or statements
  • Reveals significant incompetence or negligence in public office

Simply being "newsworthy" or attracting audience attention is not the same as being in the public interest.

Module 3: Protect Confidential Sources

Protecting confidential sources is a core professional obligation for journalists and news organisations. Without the ability to offer confidentiality, many people would not come forward with information that is in the public interest.

Best Practice — Protecting Sources

  • Only collect source information that is necessary. Do not ask for, record, or store more personal information about a source than you actually need.
  • Use secure communication channels. Signal or other encrypted messaging tools are recommended for sensitive communications.
  • Do not record source details in shared systems. Keep source identity records offline, secure, and separate from the story file.
  • Be careful with metadata. Digital files can contain metadata that reveals who created them or where they were taken. Strip metadata before publishing.
  • Train all staff. Anyone who may come into contact with source material must understand their obligations.

Module 4: Verify Information Before Publication

Publishing inaccurate information — especially about individuals — can cause serious harm and expose the organisation to legal risk. Verification is not optional.

Steps for Verifying Information

  • Identify your source. Where did the information come from? Is the source reliable?
  • Corroborate with at least one independent source. Do not publish on the basis of a single, unverified source.
  • Check documents carefully. Verify authenticity and currency of court records, company filings, etc.
  • Be careful with social media. Content is often inaccurate, out-of-date, or taken out of context.
  • Contact the person concerned (where appropriate). Give them an opportunity to respond before publication.
  • Double-check names, titles, and positions. Spelling a person's name wrong is a common but avoidable error.

Module 5: Be Transparent About Data Use

Audiences trust news organisations with their personal information. Transparency about how we use that data is both a legal obligation and a trust-building measure.

Your Transparency Obligations

1. Have a clear, accessible privacy policy. It should explain what personal information is collected, why, how it is used, and who it may be disclosed to.

2. Notify when collecting personal information. Individuals should be informed when their personal information is being collected.

3. Only use information for the stated purpose. This is a core requirement of APP 6.

4. Be careful with third-party tools. Many news websites use third-party tools that collect user data. Understand what these tools collect.

Module 6: Privacy Compliance Checklist

Use this checklist before publishing any material that involves personal information or touches on privacy.

⚠️ When in doubt — ask. If you are unsure about a privacy issue, seek guidance from an editor or privacy officer before publishing.

DO — Privacy Compliance

ActionWhy It Matters
Seek guidance when unsure about a privacy issueGetting a second opinion helps avoid mistakes.
Verify information before publishing, especially personal detailsVerification is a core professional duty.
Consider public interest — is there a clear justification?The justification must be real, not just "it's newsworthy."
Take extra care with children and vulnerable peopleChildren have a stronger right to privacy.
Protect confidential sources — use secure channelsSource protection is an ethical and sometimes legal obligation.
Be transparent about how you collected and will use informationTransparency builds audience trust and is a legal requirement.
Offer a response opportunity to people who are the subject of coverageFairness and accuracy; reduces legal risk.
Correct inaccuracies promptly if you publish something wrongPrompt correction demonstrates professionalism.

DON'T — Privacy Compliance

Avoid ThisWhat Could Go Wrong
Assume all personal information can be published just because it was obtained legallyLegal acquisition ≠ free to publish. Privacy principles still apply.
Use deception or surreptitious methods without public interest justificationMay breach ethical standards and legal obligations.
Harass or photograph people in places where privacy is expectedCan breach privacy laws and APC principles.
Publish sensitive personal information without clear public interest justificationSensitive information has higher protection under privacy law.
Share confidential source information with people who don't need to knowBreaches confidentiality and may have legal consequences.
Collect or store more personal information than you needExcessive data collection creates unnecessary privacy risk.
Assume the journalism exemption gives you a "free pass" on privacyThe exemption is conditional and does not remove all legal risk.
Ignore a subpoena or court orderIgnoring a court order can result in contempt of court.
